## PART 2 — Voice characterisation

### Sentence length and rhythm
Short-to-medium declaratives, typically 8–22 words. The dominant rhythm is **a claim followed by a warm qualifier**: "Yes, put plenty of ice into your Aperol Spritz. It sounds odd, but the more ice, the less diluted your drink will become."

**Fragments are used, but only as headers or exclamations** — "Rituale unico e inimitabile", "NEW LOOK, SAME BELOVED TASTE", "3-2-1, go!". Body copy is almost never fragmented. Aperol does not write the clipped, verbless sentence as a *style* — when a fragment appears it is doing a job (a heading, a chant, a mnemonic).

**Punctuation habits:** heavy comma use for additive lists ("good food, conversation, music and Aperol Spritz"); em-dashes and en-dashes used sparingly and warmly ("– and everywhere! –"); **exclamation marks are permitted and used** ("3-2-1, go!", "Prepara il perfetto Aperol Spritz!"); semicolons appear occasionally in heritage copy. Never uses colons for dramatic reveal. **No sentence-initial "And" or "But" as a rhetorical device** — a marked contrast with the audited site.

### Person and address
**Second person, consistently.** "you can enjoy it any time of the year", "your Aperol Spritz", "you won't be alone if you do", "you can add as many as you like". The "you" is a **guest being hosted** — a person making a choice, being reassured.

**First person plural is the brand as host and advisor:** "We'd recommend using a brut prosecco", "We suggest a slice of orange rather than a wedge". Note the hedge — "we'd recommend", "we suggest" — never "you must".

**Imperative is reserved almost exclusively for the recipe** — "Fill", "Pour", "Add", "Top up", "Garnish" — plus the responsibility lockup ("Enjoy Aperol Responsibly"). Outside a recipe, Aperol does not command.

### Recurring vocabulary
- **Colour:** bright orange, beautiful light orange, bright-orange hue, vibrant, the orange wave, paint the town orange
- **Taste:** bittersweet (the single most load-bearing word), delightfully bittersweet, bitter-sweet, perfect bitter taste, citrus notes, herbal, floral, herbaceous, aromatic herbs, roots, wood and fruits, refreshing zing, lively brightness
- **Character:** iconic, unique, inimitable, original, signature, quintessential, perfect, simple
- **Social:** together, togetherness, conviviality, in good company, friends, connections, community, shared, gathered, moments, ritual, aperitivo, tradition
- **Feeling:** joy, joyful, magic moments, cheerful, vibrant, relax, slow down
- **Heritage:** 1919, Barbieri brothers, Veneto, Padua, Venice, secret recipe, seven years, unchanged for over a century

**Adjective density is high and unembarrassed.** "fresh tasting, sophisticated and well-rounded", "delightfully bittersweet", "bold and flavorsome". Aperol stacks positive adjectives without irony.

### What they claim — and what they conspicuously never claim

**They claim:**
- Colour, flavour, and ingredients ("bittersweet", "citrus notes", "herbs and roots")
- Provenance and date (1919, Barbieri brothers, Veneto)
- Ranking, with footnoted sourcing ("#1 cocktail in Italy*", "ranked 9th … according to the Drinks International 2023")
- Ritual and occasion ("before a meal", "in good company", "any time of the year")
- ABV **as a spec only** ("11% alc./vol. (22 Proof)"); Italian copy says "bassa gradazione alcolica" — *low* alcohol content, positioned as a virtue
- Serving mechanics ("the more ice, the less diluted")

**They never claim:**
- **Any physiological effect.** Nothing about absorption, bloodstream, gastric emptying, salivation, digestion, or what the drink does to a body. The word "aperitivo" carries all the appetite meaning implicitly — they never cash it out mechanistically.
- **Intoxication, or drinking more than one.** No copy anywhere frames volume, pace, or a second drink.
- **Health, wellness, or benefit.**
- **Sensory "effect" scoring.** Taste is described as pleasure, never as stimulus-response.
- **The botanicals.** "a secret recipe", "precious primary components" — the recipe is guarded and the guarding is *itself* brand copy.

### Bitter / orange / aperitivo vocabulary handling
**Bitterness is always bound to sweetness.** The compound "bittersweet" does the work; standalone "bitter" appears only as a category noun ("an Italian bitter apéritif") or with a positive modifier ("a perfect bitter taste"). When bitterness is compared, it's softened: Campari has "a heavier weighting on the bitter elements, but it's a delicious alternative."

**Orange is colour first, flavour second, and identity above both.** Orange is the brand's visual property — "the orange wave", "paint the town orange". Aperol never reduces the orange to a chemical or a garnish function.

**"Aperitivo" is cultural, never functional.** It means Venice, bàcari, cicchetti, before a meal, good company. Aperol never explains the physiological rationale for an aperitif, even though it is the entire etymology.

### Emotional register
The copy engineers **belonging and permission**. Two moves recur:

1. **Reassurance** — "you won't be alone if you do", "It sounds odd, but…", "you can add as many as you like", "it's a delicious alternative". The reader is never wrong.
2. **Warm authority** — the brand knows the right answer (brut prosecco, slice not wedge) but delivers it as a recommendation between friends.

The target feeling is **easy, unhurried, sociable, slightly Italian** — "rallentare, staccare la spina" (slow down, unplug). Critically, **the feeling lives in the room, not in the glass.** The drink is the occasion's token. Aperol sells the table, not the liquid.

### Legal / regulatory tics
- "Enjoy Aperol Responsibly" is a **fixed global lockup**, present on every regional site including Italian, untranslated.
- ABV is a spec, never an experience.
- Rankings carry asterisked footnotes and named third-party sources.
- Recipe measures are given in parts *and* ml *and* oz, with IBA endorsement invoked ("International Bartenders Association approved").
- Regional sites operate age-affirmation gating; the responsibility line sits in the persistent footer, not buried.

### DO / DON'T rules

| # | DO | DON'T | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **DO bind bitter to sweet.** Use "bittersweet" as one word. | Don't let "bitter" stand alone as a sensation being analysed. | "delightfully bittersweet flavor"; "more bittersweet than sugary sweet" |
| 2 | **DO address the reader as "you", as a guest.** | Don't address "you" as a test subject something is happening *to*. | "you can enjoy it any time of the year"; "your Aperol Spritz" |
| 3 | **DO hedge advice as recommendation.** | Don't issue verdicts about the reader. | "We'd recommend a cava on the drier end of the scale" |
| 4 | **DO reserve the imperative for the recipe.** | Don't command outside a method. | "Fill a large wine glass generously with ice." |
| 5 | **DO reassure after any complication.** | Don't leave a surprising fact sitting cold. | "It sounds odd, but the more ice, the less diluted your drink will become." |
| 6 | **DO treat orange as identity and colour.** | Don't reduce orange to a chemical, an additive, or a percentage. | "the vibrant orange wave"; "beautiful light orange color" |
| 7 | **DO keep the recipe secret and say so.** | Don't name or speculate about botanicals. | "a secret recipe that has remained unchanged for over a century" |
| 8 | **DO locate the feeling in the company.** | Don't locate the feeling in the reader's physiology. | "Just make sure it's in good company." |

### Where the voice ALIGNS with Aperol

1. **Second person, sustained.** "what the drink actually does to you", "before you have swallowed anything". Aperol is also a "you" brand. The grammar matches even where the intent doesn't.
2. **Orange as the hero.** "Sight — the loudest thing about the drink", colour scoring the highest single effect. This is *Aperol's own claim*, arrived at independently — "the vibrant orange wave", "bright orange hue". Strongest alignment on the page.
3. **Respect for the secret recipe.** "Nothing here touches the botanicals." / "Gentian, quinine and rhubarb. Protected recipe." The site declines to model the guarded formula and says so — exactly Aperol's own "a secret recipe that has remained unchanged for over a century."
4. **Sourcing discipline.** "These timings are derived, not measured." / "Stated in the file" vs "Inferred". Aperol footnotes its rankings; the site footnotes its data. Same instinct.
5. **The 3-2-1 ratio and the serve.** "Three liquids, ice, a slice of orange" matches the canonical serve. "Watering down" echoes Aperol's own "the more ice, the less diluted your drink will become" — the site is elaborating an idea Aperol already publishes.
6. **Aperitivo-as-appetite is on-concept.** "makes it an aperitif rather than a soft drink with alcohol in it" reaches Aperol's own conclusion — "an Italian bitter apéritif, designed for sipping … before a meal." The *conclusion* is on-brand; the *mechanism* is not (see below).

### Where it DIVERGES — deliberate and defensible

These are the analytical register doing its job. **Do not flatten these.**

- **Fragments and clipped declaratives.** "Three liquids, ice, a slice of orange." Off-voice for Aperol, correct for a forensic explainer. Keep.
- **Contrarian framing of the recipe.** "it is the least useful thing you can know about the drink." Aperol would never disparage its own ratio — but the whole page is built on the volume-vs-impact inversion. Keep.
- **Numeric scoring.** 8.2 / 4.8, "eleven effects, three groups". Alien to Aperol, but it is the premise. Keep.
- **The competitor comparison per se.** Aperol never names a competitor; a data explainer legitimately can. Keep the section — but see the fixes below.
- **Cool, unadorned nouns** ("the pour", "the glass", "the file"). A defensible counterweight to Aperol's adjective stacking. Keep.
- **Naming E110/E124.** Colourants are declared on-label and legally public; this is not the secret recipe. Defensible — though the *framing* needs work (see P4).

### Where it DIVERGES — off-brand or a legal/brand problem

Ranked by severity.

**SEVERITY 1 — Encouraging additional consumption.** "**which is the row that keeps you drinking a second one**". This is copy on an Aperol-logo'd page describing a mechanism by which the product induces further drinking. It violates essentially every alcohol marketing code (encouraging excessive/continued consumption, attributing behavioural compulsion to the product). This is the single line most likely to cause a real problem.

**SEVERITY 2 — Pharmacological framing of alcohol delivery.** "Speed of absorption", "**The alcohol gets in fast and drops away just as quickly**", "**Eleven percent ABV in a wide glass, with CO2 speeding gastric emptying**", "how fast the alcohol arrives", "**Delivery is brisk but controlled**", "**a stimulant serve**", "**a delivery vehicle**", "it holds its strength from the first sip to the last". Alcohol codes prohibit claims about the rate or efficiency of alcohol delivery and prohibit stimulant claims. "Delivery vehicle" and "stimulant serve" are the vocabulary of a drug, not an aperitivo. Aperol's own position is the opposite — "bassa gradazione alcolica", low alcohol, and never a word on onset.

**SEVERITY 3 — Competitor comparison framed as superiority on delivery.** "Peroni is lower on every one" combined with "Absorption peaks at fifty minutes against the Spritz at thirty" reads as: *our drink gets alcohol into you faster than theirs.* An intentional comparison is fine; a comparison whose axis is alcohol uptake speed is not.

**SEVERITY 4 — Bodily/digestive mechanism as consumer copy.** "salivation and gastric priming", "**have you salivating and your stomach getting ready for food**", "trigeminal", "viscosity of the syrup". Aperol carries the same idea entirely through culture ("before a meal", "cicchetti", "in good company"). The clinical register makes an appetitivo sound like a dosage.

**SEVERITY 5 — The colourant framing.** "E110 and E124… **The cheapest lever on the page.**" Naming the additives is defensible; calling Aperol's defining orange a cheap trick that does "seven percent of the work" is a gift to the "it's just food dye" discourse and sits directly against "beautiful light orange color".

**SEVERITY 6 — No age gate and no responsible-drinking line.** Confirmed by inspection: `index.html` contains no 18+/21+ affirmation, no "Enjoy Responsibly", no footer legal. The page uses the Aperol wordmark and brand orange (`#fe5000`) throughout. Every Aperol property carries "Enjoy Aperol Responsibly" — including the Italian site, untranslated. **This is a compliance gap, not a tone note.**

**SEVERITY 7 — Reductive product framing.** "a soft drink with alcohol in it", "the syrup", "**it does the job**", "asking nothing of you". "Syrup" in particular is the word Aperol's detractors use.

**SEVERITY 8 — Copy defects.** "Hearing — barely there. it fizzes, and that is all" (lowercase after full stop). "Bright orange and bittersweet, Aperol Spritz brings **a iconic** twist" is Aperol's own typo, not the site's — but the site's lowercase "it" is its own.

## Summary of compliance-critical items

Three items are not stylistic and should be treated as blockers on an Aperol-branded property:

1. **"the row that keeps you drinking a second one"** — encourages continued consumption.
2. **Alcohol-delivery-rate copy** — "speed of absorption", "the alcohol gets in fast", "CO2 speeding gastric emptying", "stimulant serve", "delivery vehicle", "delivery is brisk".
3. **Missing age gate and missing "Enjoy Aperol Responsibly"** — universal on every Aperol property.

Everything else is a matter of degree, and the site's analytical register is a legitimate and interesting contrast to Aperol's warmth — worth preserving deliberately.
